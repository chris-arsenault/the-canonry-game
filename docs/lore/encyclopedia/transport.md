# Transport

*Archivist's register: kind entry, general.*

A transport is a named vessel, vehicle, or moving structure that has earned its
name. It is what carries the work that ordinary penguins cannot carry alone: a
fleet's grain, a battleship's escalation, a scout's news, a citadel's weight. A
transport is not a tool. A tool can be replaced. A transport has a story, a
captain, a fate, and a reputation that follows it through every port that
recognizes its shape.

Penguins debate whether transports have wills. Most argue that the will lives in
the captain and crew. Some Aurora records, written carefully and read more
carefully, treat a transport's named history as agency in its own right. The
argument has not been settled and will not be.

## Common Sorts

- **Trade** transports carry goods over routes too long, too fragile, or too
  organized for individual couriers. Their history is ledger-deep. The Icebound
  Exchange records every named trader of consequence and several whose
  consequence is in dispute.
- **Battleships** carry escalation. They were built or converted to project a
  faction's will into corridors that would otherwise refuse it. Their reputation
  does work that their hulls do not.
- **Scouts** carry information. They go where charters do not yet reach and
  return with what the council needs and what it would have preferred not to
  know.
- **Citadels** carry ground. They are slow, patient, fortified, and they anchor
  a defensive line where the line itself can no longer be trusted to remain.

## Owners And Captains

A transport is held by a faction, a council, a coalition, or — rarely,
expensively — an individual whose claim survives the institutional questions.
Ownership is rarely simple. Many named transports have served three or four
standards in their working lives. A few are remembered for refusing the standard
they were built to serve.

The captain matters. A captain does not own the transport, and the transport
does not obey only the captain, but the relationship between captain and named
hull is its own kind of bond. Captains who survive long enough to take a second
command are remembered as carrying something of the first ship into the
second.

## What A Transport Costs

Transports cost what they were built for and what they accumulate. A trade ship
that starts losing routes is a debt. A battleship that wins one too many
engagements is an obligation. A scout that returns with the wrong news is a
problem the council cannot delegate. A citadel that holds longer than the war
it was built for becomes a fortress no one knows how to demobilize.

A named transport rarely retires cleanly. It is lost, captured, sunk, scuttled,
or absorbed into a successor who carries the old name and the old debts
forward.

## Chronicle Use

Use transports when a story needs a moving thing whose history matters. The
vessel's reputation should precede it. Its arrival should change a settlement's
mood before any cargo is unloaded. Its loss should be a chronicle event, not a
logistic note.
